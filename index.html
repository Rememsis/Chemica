01 · AXE notation

Count regions around the center

A is the central atom; X counts atoms bonded to it; E counts lone pairs on it. A single, double, or triple bond each counts as one electron domain. For example, H₂O is AX₂E₂.

02 · VSEPR

Electron groups spread apart

Valence-shell electron-pair repulsion predicts a low-energy arrangement by keeping electron domains apart. Lone pairs occupy more space than bonding pairs, so they compress nearby bond angles. The molecular shape names atom positions, not lone pairs.

03 · Polarity

Bond dipoles add as vectors

Electronegativity differences make polar bonds. Their directions and the 3D shape determine whether the vectors cancel. A molecule can have polar bonds but no net dipole when symmetry cancels them, as in CO₂ and BF₃.

04 · Intermolecular forces

Attractions between molecules

  • Dispersion: present in every substance.
  • Dipole–dipole: between permanent molecular dipoles.
  • Hydrogen bonding: especially strong when H is bonded to N, O, or F and interacts with a lone pair.

These are classroom models. Partial charges, dipole magnitudes, bond angles, and 2D projections are qualitative or rounded; actual electron density and molecular behavior are quantum-mechanical. VSEPR is a useful shape predictor, not a complete theory of bonding.

01 · System & surroundings

Follow the energy boundary

The system is the reaction or process being studied. Heat that leaves the system enters its surroundings, so qsystem = −qsurroundings.

02 · Enthalpy

Heat at constant pressure

ΔH < 0 means the system releases heat (exothermic); ΔH > 0 means it absorbs heat (endothermic). The sign describes energy flow, not reaction speed.

03 · Hess’s law

Add the steps, add their heat

Enthalpy is a state function. If a target reaction is assembled from other reactions, reverse or scale those equations as needed and add their ΔH values.

04 · Entropy & spontaneity

Energy and dispersal both matter

At fixed temperature and pressure, ΔG < 0 favors the forward direction, ΔG > 0 favors the reverse, and ΔG = 0 indicates equilibrium. Spontaneous does not mean fast.

Calculator results use the values and ideal assumptions shown. Calorimetry neglects heat absorbed by the cup; standard formation enthalpies require consistent states and a correctly balanced equation. ΔG predicts thermodynamic favorability, not kinetics.

Student safety contract rules

Concise summaries of the supplied Flinn Scientific contract—not a replacement for the complete signed contract or your instructor’s directions.

01General rules

Act responsibly; follow written and verbal directions; ask when unsure. Do not begin until instructed, work alone, engage in horseplay, eat, drink, apply cosmetics, or use lab glassware for food. Keep the work area orderly, aisles clear, and know where safety equipment is located.

02Clothing & personal protection

Wear the goggles and protective clothing required by the contract and activity. Secure long hair and loose clothing, wear closed-toe shoes, and use any additional protection only as instructed.

03Conducting experiments

Prepare by reading the procedure and assigned references. Perform only authorized experiments and steps, use materials as directed, and never leave an active setup unattended unless specifically instructed.

04Handling chemicals

Read container labels and directions before use. Use only assigned chemicals and amounts; never taste or directly smell chemicals, and do not handle them without authorization. If instructed to dilute an acid, add acid to water—never water to acid. Report spills promptly and follow disposal directions.

05Accidents & injuries

Tell the instructor immediately about every accident, injury, spill, or broken item—even if it seems minor. For a chemical splash on skin or in eyes, immediately flush at the designated safety shower or eyewash for at least 15 minutes and alert the instructor.

06Glassware & equipment

Check glassware and equipment before use; do not use damaged items. Handle glass carefully, keep it away from bench edges, and use the designated container for broken glass. Operate equipment only as demonstrated.

07Heating substances

Heat only when authorized and follow the demonstrated setup. Never heat a closed container or leave heating unattended. Keep your face and others away from vessel openings, use the proper holder for hot items, and treat glassware as hot until it has cooled.

08Clean up & questions

Clean your station, wash your hands, and return equipment as directed. Dispose of chemicals, glass, and other waste only by the assigned method. If you are unsure about a step, stop and ask before continuing.

What is a chemical bond?

A chemical bond is an attractive interaction that helps hold atoms or ions together. Bonding is about the distribution and energy of electrons and nuclei—not simply atoms “wanting” a full shell. The three broad introductory models are ionic, covalent, and metallic bonding, with real substances often showing mixed character.

Important distinction

Bond type, bond polarity, molecular polarity, and intermolecular forces describe different things. A polar covalent bond is inside a molecule; hydrogen bonding and other intermolecular forces act between particles.

Ionic bonding

Oppositely charged ions attract

In the introductory model, electron transfer produces cations and anions. Their electrostatic attractions extend through a crystal lattice; an ionic solid is not usually made of separate “molecules.”

Many ionic compounds have high melting points and conduct electricity when their ions can move in a melt or solution, but not as rigid solids. Actual properties depend on the material.

Example · NaCl(s): Na⁺ and Cl⁻ in a lattice

Covalent bonding

Atoms share electron density

A covalent bond forms when atoms share electron density. One, two, or three shared pairs are commonly represented as single, double, or triple bonds. Bonds can be nonpolar or polar depending on the atoms and their electron distribution.

Covalent substances may be discrete molecules or extended networks, so their physical properties vary widely.

Examples · H₂O, O₂, diamond

Metallic bonding

Delocalized valence electrons

In metals, valence electrons are spread across many atoms rather than assigned to one pair of neighbors. This model helps explain electrical and thermal conductivity, malleability, and ductility.

Real metallic structure is more nuanced than a simple sea-of-electrons picture, but it is a useful starting model.

Examples · Cu(s), Al(s), Fe(s)

Unequal sharing

Bonding is a continuum

Electronegativity difference (ΔEN) is a useful classroom guide, not a sharp boundary that determines every bond. Small differences often correspond to more even covalent sharing; larger differences indicate more uneven sharing and ionic character. Context, structure, and measured properties matter—there is no universal cutoff that perfectly classifies every bond.

More even sharingMore ionic character
01 · Bond polarity

Uneven sharing within a bond

A polar covalent bond has an uneven electron distribution. The δ− end is toward the more electronegative atom; δ+ marks the other end.

02 · Molecular polarity

Whole-molecule shape matters

A molecule’s bond dipoles combine as vectors. A molecule can have polar bonds but no net dipole when its geometry is sufficiently symmetric, as in CO₂.

03 · Bond strength

Breaking bonds takes energy

Bond dissociation energies are energy required to break bonds in a specified chemical environment. Forming bonds releases energy; reaction enthalpy reflects the net balance.

04 · Intermolecular forces

Attractions between particles

Dispersion, dipole–dipole interactions, and hydrogen bonding are intermolecular attractions, not the same thing as ionic, covalent, or metallic bonds.

Electronegativity values and ΔEN bands are qualitative teaching aids. Bonding in real materials is quantum-mechanical and can have mixed ionic, covalent, and metallic character.

01 · Atoms

Inside the atom

Protons define the element, neutrons distinguish isotopes, and electrons determine the charge of an ion. The mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons.

02 · Patterns

The periodic table

Rows are periods and columns are groups. Elements in a group often have related valence-electron patterns and share some chemical properties.

03 · Electron shells

Bohr models

Use an element’s electron configuration to show how electrons occupy principal energy levels, then relate the outer shell to valence electrons.

04 · Valence electrons

Lewis diagrams

Represent valence electrons as dots, lone pairs as paired dots, and covalent bonds as shared pairs of electrons.

05 · Bonds

Bonding basics

Ionic bonding describes electrostatic attraction between ions, covalent bonding describes shared electron density, and metallic bonding involves delocalized electrons.

06 · Shape

Molecular shapes

VSEPR relates the arrangement of electron domains around a central atom to molecular shape. Lone pairs count as domains and can affect bond angles.

07 · Energy

Heat & energy

Calorimetry estimates heat transfer with q = mcΔT. Enthalpy tracks heat at constant pressure, while Gibbs energy describes thermodynamic favorability under stated conditions—not reaction speed.

08 · Lab

Lab-ready habits

Follow the written procedure and instructor directions, use required protective equipment, report spills or injuries immediately, and use only the designated waste stream.

09 · Matter & change

Properties & reactions

Physical properties describe a substance without changing its identity. Chemical properties describe how it can form new substances; reactions rearrange atoms while conserving each element.

Properties & reactions.

Describe what a substance is like, distinguish physical changes from chemical reactions, and recognize common reaction patterns.

2kinds of properties
6reaction patterns

Physical properties

Observed or measured without changing a substance’s chemical identity. Examples include color, density, melting point, electrical conductivity, and whether a sample is solid, liquid, or gas at a stated temperature and pressure.

Example · liquid water freezes → solid water (H₂O remains H₂O)

Chemical properties

Describe a substance’s ability to form different substances through chemical change. Examples include flammability, tendency to react with acids or oxygen, and resistance to corrosion.

Example · iron can react with oxygen and water to form rust

Physical change or chemical reaction?

A change in appearance or state alone does not mean a new substance formed. Look for changes in chemical composition.

Physical change

Composition stays the same

Form, size, or state changes, but the particles remain the same substance. Many physical changes can be reversed by another physical process.

Examples · ice melting · cutting copper · salt dissolving in water

Chemical reaction

New substances form

Atoms are rearranged as bonds break and form. The products have different chemical compositions and properties from the reactants.

Examples · iron rusting · food digesting · fuel burning

Clues to investigate

Evidence is contextual

A persistent color change, gas formation, a precipitate, or energy transfer may be evidence of a reaction. None proves a chemical reaction by itself: boiling makes bubbles, and mixing can change temperature without forming a new substance.

Ask · did the substances’ chemical identities change?

Common reaction patterns

These patterns help organize reactions, but not every reaction fits one introductory category perfectly.

Synthesis

Several reactants form a product

Two or more reactants combine to make a compound.

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Decomposition

One reactant forms simpler products

A compound breaks down, sometimes with heat, light, electricity, or a catalyst.

2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂

Single replacement

An element replaces another

An element reacts with a compound to form a new compound and a different element, when the reaction is favorable.

Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂

Double replacement

Ions exchange partners

Two ionic compounds exchange ions in solution; a reaction proceeds when a product such as a precipitate, water, or gas forms.

AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃

Combustion

Reaction with an oxidizer

In complete combustion of a hydrocarbon in sufficient oxygen, the products are carbon dioxide and water. Other conditions can produce different products.

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Acid–base neutralization

Acid and base react

A common aqueous neutralization produces water and a dissolved ionic compound called a salt.

HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O
Reading equations: Reactants are written to the left of the arrow and products to the right. A balanced equation has the same number of each kind of atom on both sides; balance by changing coefficients, never the chemical formulas.
